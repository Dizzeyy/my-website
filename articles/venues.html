Dizzeyy's Videogame Lecture Lab

Venues

November 11, 2026

I read a book once called 'The Art of Game Design', by Jesse Schell.
It's an interesting book.
Basically it's a bunch of game design ideas slapped together,
formatted according to a map of theoretical player experience.
It's really cool,
and brimming, just bursting at the seems, absolutely FILLED to its sides being squeezed taught
with ideas.
Some of those ideas, I'll be honest, I've thought about since reading the book.
I'm going to take one of those ideas here,
extrapolate on it,
explain it.
Here's the main thing:
"venues."

The idea is, when you're designing a game you are not just designing an experience in a vacuum.
Each video game has a place where it is played.
A nintendo DS is played in variable locations,
but a nintendo wii will be played on a TV.
And that TV is probably in a relatively public place.
That relatively public place is probably not the same kind of place where you have a computer,
especially a super-juiced-up one, your super-riced lenovo laptop with a custom linux distro that's plugged into three backup generators and a 4x size rubber duck.
(For coding purposes.)

See, Jesse Schell figured out that one useful perspective in video gaming is to look at the venue games are played in.
He realized that you could categorize them by how private or how public they were.
For instance, he talks about one venue that hasn't changed much in human history: "the workbench."
A place of solitude, where a single person works on some kind of craft.
A desk in a garage, or a personal workspace in an office.
Or, the place where you have a computer and play factorio.

A much different venue than what Schell calls "the hearth", which is the communal area where people living together come to share warmth.
The TV, he argues, is much like a modern hearth - and games played in the hearth venue are things like mario kart, and snipperclips.
Shared experiences, shared stories.

Is this a hard and fast rule? No. Does every game have to slot into some perfect venue category? I don't think so.
Especially not handheld games, which can be played anywhere from a train to the venue Schell would call "the reading nook", a private place to do something low-effort and rejuvenative
(as opposed to the workbench, which is for focused, sometimes high-octane work).

And some games do feel like work,
or as I've heard them described "fun chores" -
factorio, minecraft.
And some games do feel like a shared experience around a communal place -
super smash brothers, wii sports.
And some games are definitely private experiences -
Ace Attorney, HuniePop.

And this way of looking at experiences and games has stuck with me,
but especially the hearth.
See, a communal living area with some point of interest between people
(or at least at some point in the room)
is an interesting way to describe a room.
But it's not a perspective that I'd seen or heard before.
And I thought it was neat.

That's basically what the book is full of,
and I'm not trying to sell you copies of his book,
I don't know the guy, if you want to talk to him go to Pennsylvania.
