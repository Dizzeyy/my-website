Context and The Witness

November 13, 2026

The thing about The Witness is Joseph Anderson made a fucking 41-minute video about it in 2016 called "The Witness - A Great Game You Shouldn't Play".

The thesis of his video is that the game sucks.

So me, being a person who watched Joseph Anderson's video and thinking he had made a point, thought to myself, 'hmm, better not play that game.'

And then I played that game.

So the thing is, when you come at a game intended for chill audiences with an intensity of a coke-crazed gerbil, I think you may be in for a disappointment.

Conversely, if you come at a game expecting DOOM but you get minesweeper, you may be in for a disappointment.

Games have an emotional octane level they try to get across in their tone, storytelling, music, atmosphere, dialogue, and game mechanics. Slow, methodical puzzles and thoughtful turn-based combat invite you to come along and try to slow down. Fast, stylistic firearm gauntlets and snappy fighting arenas invite you to breathe fast and respond with alacrity.

But you have to know that.

Setting up expectations is on both the game developer and the player. I'm not taking sides here. If the player doesn't do five seconds of homework to figure out what to expect or the develop fucks up in establishing expectations, oh well. I am not the moral arbiter of telling players not to pull their dick out for a non-hentai game, but I will become the moral arbiter of saying that it's stupid to open up a hentai game and not pull your dick out.

If you, the player, are reading this out of mere curiosity and interest and are enjoying it...well then keep reading!

If you, the player, are reading this out of a desire to learn something about game design, well then, this is it:

Game design is about creating an experience of a certain emotional timbre. You need to show the player what that timbre will be.

An interesting note about common criticism for gaming is that a games 'intended feel' is often left out.

It's ridiculous.

Here's a hypothetical to show you what I am talking about.

Someone sits down at their desk one morning.

"Ah," they say to themselves. "Today, I will make the boringest of all boring games. It will be repetitive, and calming, and it will put your mind in a state of numbness."

So they get started working on State of Numbness, a boring game to rival all boring games.

They sit in a plastic white chair at a colorless table in an office whose only element not in greyscale is a slightly off-purple rock in a corner.

They drink tea with no additives, from a mug with no pictures or words,

programming on their Debian computer.

They finish the game exactly on schedule.

They advertise it with a monotone grey background and the title of the game in Calibri.

They present a video on Steam showing five minutes of gameplay, where the player sits on a bench in a field.

Cut to a few months later.

One day a guy named Max comes along and downloads State of Numbness

And he plays it.

And he talks to his work friends later that week around the water cooler in the office.

"Say," he says. "Anybody play a game called State of Numbness?"

"Why, no," they respond calmly. "What is it?"

"Well," Max responds calmly. "It's the fucking worst piece of shit game I ever fucking played."

"Oh," they say. "Well alright."

CONTEXT

Games deserve context when being talked about.

They deserve to be introduced in the way the developer hopes they got introduced.

Developers obviously have an idea for how the game should feel. Elevator pitches exist for a reason - they are excellent ways of quickly communicating expectations. That's all they are.

What is ESPECIALLY difficult is communicating how a game falls outside of the expectations it provokes.

Let me give you an example.

Halo.

If you played halo, you know that the game was a departure in design from military shooters.

It also feels like a departure in design from classic arcade science-fiction games.

But it is a science-fiction military shooter. And if you tell someone that, they might think they get what halo is.

But Halo feels so different than every other military shooter. That term, to me at least, feels unfair.

And playing Halo is not playing the campaign. It's playing custom games where you run around with a gravity hammer at 300% speed and bonk each other while driving mongooses.

Halo is like two games. The narrative of Master Chief and the space war, and the actually fun game where you play a bunch of different game modes in customizeable (and very beautiful) multiplayer maps.

You need to separate one from the other, but the game is marketed as a single military action game,

the fact that the multiplayer is what made it endure notwithstanding.

I'm sure there are more than a few people who downloaded a game with pretty anime women or exciting cinematic trailers only to be disappointed in a weird RTS resource management game that has neither tits nor fights.

This is where it gets tricky, because now we are in the field of guessing who knows what to expect.

I know of a few resources that are helpful, but by far the most helpful resource is just watching someone else playing the fucking game.

Game critics are a resource, but like a tool they have to be used correctly.

You have to get a game critic whose tastes are like your own. Otherwise, you will listen to someone convincing you not to play a game that you might have enjoyed.

You can also learn about game studios and developers. Each studio and developer, like any artist or creative group, comes to make things in a certain style.

A Supergiant game has a recognizeable art style and quality of gameplay that you can expect from the studio.

Nintendo is not a single team but multiple groups with different names and different people that focus on specific niches. Nintendo EPD Production Group 8 is the name of the group that works on the 3D mario games such as Super Mario Odyssey. When EPD Production Group 8 develops a game, you can expect a high degree of polish, even if it isn't a massive title. They also developed

Captain Toad: Treasure Tracker

and Donkey Kong Bananza

You can also come to know what to expect from specific creators, such as:

Michel Ancel, creator of the Rayman franchise and lead designer of both Origins and Legends. Ancel champions high degrees of freedom in his games.

Lucas Pope, creator of Papers, Please and Return of the Obra Dinn. Pope's core interest is not in programming, but in developing GUI tools. His games are essentially built out of UX interfaces.

Hidetaka Miyazaki, important designer in FROM Software's Dark Souls games. His history-and-mythology-inspired games are known for being difficult because of his philosophy that players should feel that they overcame tremendous odds. He also prefers to make games without adjustable difficulty, and creates in-game stories that can be interpreted by the player rather than explicitly told.

John Carmack, co-founder of id software with John Romero and lead programmer of the foundational DOOM, Quake, and Wolfenstein. I'll give you his philosophy in a quote of his:

“Story in a game is like story in a porn movie. It's expected to be there, but it's not important." -John Carmack

And then there's Tim Schafer, creator of Grim Fandango and Psychonauts.

If Hidetaka Miyazaki comes out with a game and I am a gamer who is trying to get his expectations up to par, I can be confident that it will be difficult, depressing, and historical-themed.

If John Carmack or John Romero made another game, it would probably be a 3D action shooter.

You see what I'm getting at.

Remember, games are like food. There is a mind-boggling variety, and lots of different tastes.

But if someone eats fourteen ghost pepper sausages then complains about how their toilet now has a scoville rating, the famous reply will always be:

"Well, what did you expect?"

That's the thing with games.

You came at a Jonathon Blow puzzle game.

What you got was a slow, lovely, cerebral puzzle game with some existential themes tied into the commentary.

And now you're complaining that the game didn't have a concrete point...

Well, what did you expect?
