On Multiplayer Maps

November 21, 2026

I set out in the summer of 2023 to find out how good maps were designed. I looked at dozens of examples across the most popular shooters in the last several decades.

I have seen many maps.

I found out what the popular ones had in common. The most popular multiplayer maps in any game are the ones that are small relative to the rest of the game’s map roster, symmetrical along one axis, and simple. Dead - basic - simple.

There is an outlier, and that is battle royale games. In these games, the maps are enormous, the player starts at one point in the map, and the killzone shrinks to an unpredictable point in the map.

But the rest - games I’ll call ‘battle arena’ games - are games where the multiplayer games are set in closed arenas designed for smaller numbers of players, usually less than twenty. These maps are often especially commonly sized for two teams of players or free-for-alls. Some of the most popular examples of battle arena games in their respective eras and genres are Quake, Halo, Call of Duty, Team Fortress 2, Rainbow Six: Siege, Overwatch, Counter Strike, Splatoon, Valorant, Battlefront, and Goldeneye.

The most popular maps among Call of Duty, Team Fortress 2, Halo, and Counter-Strike are as follows:

  • Nuketown
  • 2Fort
  • Blood Gulch
  • Valhalla
  • Beaver Creek
  • Shipment
  • Dust II

When I set out to find what made maps good, I was prepared to write an almanac of rules relating to health packs, distance, verticality, lanes, and designing maps to accommodate each of a game’s different class abilities. I even looked at maps from games that weren’t arena shooters, such as Dota 2. These other fancy ideas apply, but apart from distance and possibly lanes, they are rather irrelevant.

Make it small, simple, and symmetrical.

This is how to make an arena shooter’s map good.
